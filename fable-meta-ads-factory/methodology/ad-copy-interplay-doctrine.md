# The Interplay Doctrine — every ad element plays a different note

## What problem this solves

Most Meta ad units waste their own real estate. A single-image feed ad has **four
independent text surfaces** — the headline baked into the image, the primary text
(the caption above the image), the ad headline (the bold line under the image next
to the button), and the description (the small grey line under that). The lazy
default is to make all four say roughly the same thing. When every surface
restates one message, three of your four surfaces are dead weight; the ad has one
idea and repeats it, so it reads as thin and "advertise-y."

The Interplay Doctrine fixes that: **each element plays a different note of the
same chord.** No two surfaces say the same thing. Together they resolve into one
complete thought — and because each surface advances the idea instead of echoing
it, the ad feels like a single, tight argument rather than a slogan shouted four
times.

The signature move is a **call-and-response** between the image headline and the
ad headline: the image opens a loop ("Nobody pays you for this"), and the ad
headline — sitting right next to the button — snaps it shut ("We do."). The
reader's eye travels image → caption → headline → button, and the payoff lands
exactly where the click is.

---

## The four surfaces and their assigned roles

Think of the unit as one sentence spread across four instruments. Give each a job
and forbid it from doing any other job:

| Surface | Where it appears | Its ONE job |
|---|---|---|
| **Image headline** (baked into the picture) | Inside the creative | **Open the loop.** Name the tension/recognition. Do NOT resolve it. |
| **Primary text (first line)** | Caption, above the image | **Hook.** A curiosity line that must NOT pay itself off. Earns the "See more" tap. |
| **Ad headline** | Bold line under image, beside the button | **Answer the image headline.** Resolve the loop, hand off to the button. |
| **Description** | Small grey line under the headline | **Kicker.** One extra detail — a spec, a qualifier, a location. Never a repeat. |

Two supporting principles make it work:

- **Recognition before persuasion.** The image should be a scene from the
  reader's *actual day* — something they've literally seen — so the ad earns a
  flicker of "that's my life" before it asks for anything. Recognition is the
  cheapest attention you can buy; a stock/hero shot spends it, a real-looking
  moment banks it.
- **The curiosity gap lives in the caption.** The first visible line of primary
  text (the part shown before "See more") must open a question it does not
  answer. If the first line resolves, there is no reason to expand the caption.

## The call-and-response mechanic (the core trick)

The highest-leverage version of interplay is a two-beat exchange between the two
headlines:

1. **Image headline states a hard, uncomfortable truth** — a loop the reader
   can't close on their own.
2. **Ad headline answers it** in the reader's ear, right at the button, in as
   few words as possible.

The classic pattern is a **negation → correction**:

> **Image (baked):** NOBODY PAYS YOU FOR SPOTTING THE PROBLEM.
> **Ad headline:** We do. *(right next to the button)*

That "We do." does three things at once: it resolves the tension the image
created, it names *you* as the answer, and it points at the click. The reader
supplied the emotional charge (frustration at being unpaid); you supplied the
two-word release. Call-and-response converts a passive scroll into a completed
thought — and a completed thought is what gets acted on.

Other call-and-response shapes that follow the same logic:

- **Cost → reframe.** Image: "THE EASY FIX WAS THE CHEAP PART." Ad headline:
  "The real job is worth more."
- **Position → payoff.** Image: "YOU'RE ALWAYS FIRST ON SCENE." Ad headline:
  "Being first just started paying."
- **Count → change.** Image: "YOU'VE SEEN THIS A HUNDRED TIMES." Ad headline:
  "This time it pays you."

In every case the image poses; the headline answers. Never let the image *also*
answer itself — that collapses the loop and kills the response.

## A generic worked example (invented)

Suppose you sell a **rapid repricing tool to independent hardware-store owners**.
Here's a unit built strictly by the doctrine. Notice no two surfaces repeat.

**Creative (image):** a photo of a shelf tag with yesterday's price still on it,
a competitor's flyer half-visible beside it undercutting it. **Baked headline:**

> YOUR SHELF STILL SELLS AT LAST MONTH'S PRICE.

*(Image role: recognition — every owner has felt this — plus it opens the loop:
"…and?")*

**Primary text (first line, the hook — must not resolve):**

> The chain across the street changes prices twice a day. You change yours when
> you get a spare afternoon.

*(Hook role: names the asymmetry, opens a question — "so what do I do about it?" —
without answering. This earns the "See more.")*

**Rest of primary text (after "See more"):**

> Repricer watches your competitors and rewrites your shelf tags every morning
> before you unlock the door. You set the floor; it does the rest. Ten stores in
> your region already run it.

**Ad headline (answers the image, at the button):**

> Reprice While You Sleep

*(Answer role: it resolves the image's "last month's price" tension directly —
you're no longer the one changing tags on a spare afternoon.)*

**Description (kicker — one new fact, no repeat):**

> Setup in an afternoon · Independent stores only

Cover any single surface and the unit feels incomplete — that's the test that it's
working:

- Hide the **image** and you lose the recognition; the copy floats without a scene.
- Hide the **first line** and there's no reason to expand the caption.
- Hide the **ad headline** and the image's loop never closes — the reader is left
  hanging at the button.
- Hide the **description** and you lose the qualifier that makes it feel real
  ("independent stores only").

## Rules of the doctrine (checklist)

- [ ] **No two surfaces say the same thing.** If the ad headline paraphrases the
      image headline, rewrite one of them.
- [ ] **The image opens; the headline answers.** Assign the loop-open to the image
      and the loop-close to the ad headline, not the reverse.
- [ ] **The first line of primary text is a hook, not a summary.** It must leave a
      question open before "See more."
- [ ] **The image is a scene from the reader's real day.** Recognition first.
      Prefer a moment they've literally lived over a polished hero shot.
- [ ] **The description is a kicker.** New information only — a spec, a
      qualifier, a location. Never a restatement.
- [ ] **The cover test.** Hide any one element; the ad should feel incomplete. If
      hiding an element changes nothing, that element was redundant — fix it.
- [ ] **Keep the "asking" surface calm.** The image can state the hard truth
      loudly; the ad headline that answers should be short and plain (often two to
      four words). The contrast is what makes the response land.

---

## Why this beats "message match" advertising

Conventional advice says make every element reinforce one message ("message
match"). Reinforcement is fine for a billboard glimpsed for one second. But a feed
ad is *read*, in sequence, by a thumb that's deciding whether to keep scrolling at
every surface. Interplay respects that sequence: it gives the reader a reason to
move from image → caption → headline → button, because each step **adds** rather
than repeats. Reinforcement asks the reader to hear the same note four times;
interplay lets them hear a chord resolve. One is louder. The other is better.

A useful side effect: because the offer's hardest specifics (prices, numbers,
claims) can live on the **ad headline** rather than baked into the image, the
image itself stays clean — a scene and a plain, tension-naming line. That keeps
the most-scrutinized surface (the creative) simple, and puts the "answer" where
the click is. Good structure and clean creatives, from the same doctrine.
