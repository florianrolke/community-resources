# Meta Compliance Playbook — recruiting-style / paid-opportunity lead ads

## What problem this solves

If you run Meta lead ads that offer people money to do something — a referral fee,
a paid partnership, a "get paid when X happens" arrangement — you are standing on
top of **three policy tripwires at once**:

1. Meta may auto-classify your ad as **Employment** (a Special Ad Category), which
   strips most of your targeting.
2. You want to reach people by **occupation and life situation** (e.g. "recently
   relocated"), some of which brushes against Meta's personal-attributes rules.
3. You are putting **dollar figures** in ads, which can trip earnings-claim
   classifiers.

Each is survivable with the right framing. Each can get an ad account restricted
if handled lazily. This is a reusable checklist for staying on the right side of
all three when you're advertising a paid opportunity to a niche trade or
professional audience. It is written generically — adapt the specifics to your
own offer.

> **Worked example used throughout:** a small local services company that pays
> independent trade professionals a **referral fee** — a fixed amount when they
> send a qualified lead, and a larger amount when the resulting job closes — for
> flagging work the company can do. Treat this as one concrete instance of a
> "paid-opportunity lead ad," not a template to copy verbatim.

---

## §1 · Identity hygiene (do this before anything else)

Whatever public persona/brand the campaign runs under, make it **consistent
everywhere** and make sure no unintended real name or private detail leaks:

- The Facebook Page, ad account admin persona, Instant Form, privacy policy, and
  any automated thank-you messages should all present the **same brand identity**.
- Before launch, scan the Page's **About / transparency** section and the linked
  website for anything you didn't mean to publish (a personal name on an About
  page, a stray internal reference). Page transparency shows the admin's country
  only — that's expected and fine.
- If there's a name or detail that must not appear publicly, grep every asset —
  ad copy, form fields, page bio, canned replies — for it before the first ad goes
  live. One leaked string on an About page is enough to undo the whole framing.

---

## §2 · The Employment Special-Ad-Category question (biggest structural risk)

**The risk.** Meta auto-classifies ads that read like "get paid to do X" as
**Employment** ads. If flagged, the ad must be re-declared under the Employment
Special Ad Category (SAC). That declaration **removes** age/gender targeting,
tightens location targeting (a large minimum radius), and disables most detailed
targeting. Worse, repeatedly running *undeclared* ads that reviewers consider
employment ads earns **policy strikes** on the account.

**The defensible position** for a referral/partnership offer: this is a
**business-to-business referral partnership** between two businesses, with
per-referral fees. It is not a job, a wage, or an employment relationship. That
position holds up — but only if the copy consistently *sounds* like a B2B
partnership rather than a job posting.

| Never write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| hiring, job, apply, position, work for us | partner, referral partner, partnership |
| earn extra income / side hustle / side income | referral fee, per-lead fee, revenue for your business |
| **"Apply Now"** CTA button | a partnership-appropriate CTA (e.g. **"Sign Up"**) |
| get paid to… | we pay a referral fee of $X per qualified lead |
| join our team | become a partner |

- Put the small print on the **Instant Form and landing page**: something to the
  effect of *"per-referral fees to independent professionals; a business-to-
  business partnership, not employment."* That line is your appeal evidence if an
  ad ever gets misclassified.
- **Decision: do not pre-declare Employment SAC** for a genuine B2B fee
  arrangement. Declaring it kills your targeting and is factually wrong for a
  partnership.
- **Fallback if Meta flags anyway:** appeal **once** with the B2B framing. If the
  appeal fails, run the campaign declared as Employment SAC with a broad location
  radius and lean on *creative-as-targeting* (well-written copy self-selects your
  audience, so losing SAC targeting is survivable). Do not keep re-submitting the
  same ad undeclared — that's how strikes accumulate.

## §3 · Personal attributes (the "recently relocated" and status tripwire)

Meta prohibits copy that **asserts or implies you know a personal attribute** of
the viewer — where they live now vs. before, their financial situation, their
personal circumstances. If your angle depends on the reader having recently moved,
changed jobs, or fallen on hard times, that angle must live in **stories about
someone else** or in **conditionals**, never in a claim about the reader.

- ✅ *"A tradesperson who moved here from out of state told me…"* — third-person
  story; you're describing someone else, not the reader.
- ✅ *"If you came to this city for a bigger opportunity…"* — a conditional; it
  implies no knowledge of the reader.
- ✅ *"Local plumbers:"* / *"Local HVAC techs:"* — an occupational callout to an
  audience. Keep it plural and audience-level. Occupation is not a protected
  attribute and trade callouts are ubiquitous and low-risk.
- ❌ *"Did you just move here?"* / *"New in town?"* as a direct address.
- ❌ *"Your rate dropped when you left your last city"* — implies you know their
  history.
- ❌ Anything implying financial status: *"struggling?"*, *"broke?"*,
  *"need extra cash?"*

The rule of thumb: **talk about a third person, or pose a condition — never
diagnose the reader.** Keep occupational callouts plural ("local plumbers"), not
diagnostic ("you're an underpaid plumber").

## §4 · Money claims & dollar figures

- **Fee schedule = allowed. Income projection = trouble.** Stating a
  transactional price as a fact — *"$X when you send a qualified lead, $Y when the
  job closes"* — is a fee schedule, backed by a real transaction. That is
  materially different from an **earnings claim** (*"make $3,000/month"*), which
  trips the get-rich-quick / unacceptable-business-practices classifiers.
- **Never multiply.** *"a few leads a month × $Y = …"* converts a fee into an
  income promise. Keep the arithmetic **out of ads, forms, and the landing page.**
  (You can discuss expected volume verbally on a call; just never publish the
  multiplication.)
- **Ban the hype words:** no *"guaranteed"*, no *"easy money"*, no *"risk-free"*.
- **Mind insurance-adjacent language.** If your offer touches insured work, say
  *"when the job closes,"* never *"when the claim closes."* Insurance-speak invites
  financial-services scrutiny and regulatory adjacency you don't want in writing.
- **Comparisons stay attributed, never as attacks.** If you cite what other
  programs pay, frame it as what people *report* (*"the program offered him $X"*),
  as a market fact — not as a named slam on a competitor (see §5).

---

## §5 · Competitor naming (don't)

Never name a specific competitor company or program in any ad, form, or page.
Two reasons: trademark-complaint risk, and it tips off exactly whose people you're
courting. Speak **generically** only — *"the big-company programs,"* *"what most
programs pay."* This keeps you clear of trademark disputes and keeps your strategy
quiet.

---

## §6 · Lead-ads (Instant Form) specific rules

- A **live privacy-policy URL** is mandatory on any lead form. It must exist under
  your brand's name **before** launch — treat it as a hard pre-flight item.
- **Don't request prohibited information.** No SSNs, financial-account numbers,
  health data, or other sensitive categories. Name / phone / email plus a couple
  of qualifying questions are safe.
- **Form scent must match ad scent.** The form's questions and promise have to
  match the ad — Meta reviews the form as part of the ad. A form that promises
  something different from the ad reads as a bait-and-switch.
- **Contact leads the way the form says you will.** If the form says "we'll text
  you," text them. Blasting unrelated marketing to lead-form data violates the
  lead-ads terms.

## §7 · Account-health practice

These transfer to essentially any new or low-history ad account:

- **Expect new-advertiser spend caps.** For the first several weeks, even an
  existing account with little spend history will have a **daily spend cap**.
  Don't fight it — ramp gradually.
- **A rejected ad is never resubmitted unchanged.** Edit it meaningfully, or
  appeal **once** via Account Quality. Repeated identical resubmits look like
  circumvention and trigger a restriction spiral.
- **Have a fallback creative ready.** If one unit draws a rejection (the
  likeliest culprit is dollar figures baked into the image), pause that unit, drop
  a **no-number variant** into its slot, and appeal the original in parallel.
- **Check Account Quality weekly** (Business Suite → Account Quality) even when
  ads look live — Meta sometimes limits delivery silently.
- **Keep ad ↔ form ↔ page congruent** — same offer, same numbers across all three.
  Congruence is scored.
- **One campaign edit per day, max, in week one.** Every edit resets the learning
  phase.
- **Know your kill-switch.** If the account gets restricted: **stop spending,
  appeal once, wait.** Never spin up a second ad account to route around a
  restriction — circumvention risks a permanent ban.

---

## §8 · Pre-launch checklist (run once, before the first ad goes live)

- [ ] Facebook Page + ad account healthy: Account Quality shows no restrictions.
- [ ] Page name / About / contact all match the intended brand identity; no
      unintended real names or private details anywhere (per §1).
- [ ] Privacy policy live on your site under the brand's name (required by the
      Instant Form).
- [ ] Valid payment method attached; note the current spend limit.
- [ ] Instant Form built to spec and **preview-tested on an actual phone.**
- [ ] Lead notifications ON in the Business Suite app; verify with a **test lead**
      via the Leads Center testing tool.
- [ ] Every creative passes this playbook: identity (§1), wording (§2/§4), no
      competitor names (§5).
- [ ] A no-number fallback creative is built and ready to swap in (§7).
- [ ] Kill-switch understood: if restricted, stop, appeal once, wait — never open
      a second ad account (§7).

---

## The one-paragraph version

You're advertising a paid opportunity, so Meta will look at you through three
lenses: employment, personal attributes, and money claims. Frame the offer as a
**B2B partnership** (never a job), speak about the reader's situation only in the
**third person or as a condition** (never diagnose them), state your prices as a
**fee schedule** and never as multiplied income, keep **competitor names out**,
make your **form and page match your ad**, and treat the ad account gently — ramp
spend slowly, appeal rejections once, and keep a no-number fallback in your back
pocket. Do those things and a paid-opportunity lead ad campaign runs clean.
